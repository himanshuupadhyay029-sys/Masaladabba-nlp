Masala Dabba

Teaching a computer to read an Indian ingredient list, one spice at a time.

  • Serves one NLP viva
  • Prep 538 labelled lines
  • Cook 30 epochs, CPU
  • Yield 0.95 F1

Why this dish?

A human reads 2 medium onions, finely chopped and instantly knows the amount, the thing and what to do to it. A computer sees only a string. This project trains a small named-entity recognition (NER) model to pull four things out of any ingredient line, then measures it honestly against hand-written rules.

The name: a masala dabba is the round steel spice box in every Indian kitchen, with a compartment for each spice. The model's job is to sort each line into the right compartments.

The four spices what the model extracts

  • QUANTITY haldi

    How much: 2, 1/2, 2-3, and words like a pinch, to taste.

  • UNIT elaichi

    The measure: tbsp, cup, inch, or count words like cloves in 5 cloves garlic.

  • INGREDIENT mirchi

    The whole name, adjectives included: green chilli, kashmiri red chilli powder, dhaniya patta.

  • PREP kesar

    What is done to it: finely chopped, slit, soaked overnight. Size words count too.

Ingredients what went into the project

  • 538 ingredient lines: 486 scraped from 36 recipes on Hebbar's Kitchen, Archana's Kitchen and Tarla Dalal, plus 52 hand-written messy ones (typos, Hinglish, ranges)
  • 1,788 labelled entities, following written annotation guidelines
  • 41% of lines contain a Roman-script Hindi or regional word (jeera, haldi, adrak, lehsun)
  • 1 blank English spaCy pipeline: it knew nothing about food before training
  • 1 regex baseline, written by hand, to keep the model honest
  • A laptop CPU. No GPU, no cloud

Method

  1. Gather. Scrape the ingredient lists politely (2-second pauses), keep the mess: fractions, typos, tbps.
  2. Label. Mark every quantity, unit, ingredient and prep. Write down the rules for the awkward cases first, so medium is PREP every time, not just on Mondays.
  3. Divide. Split 70 / 15 / 15 into train, dev and test, with every label present in every part. The test set is sealed until the end.
  4. Make the baseline. Regex for numbers and ranges, word lists for units and prep, "the longest leftover run of words" for the ingredient.
  5. Temper the model. Train for 30 epochs. Each token becomes numbers from its spelling and shape, a small network peeks at its neighbours, and the NER head decides token by token. Keep the checkpoint with the best dev score.
  6. Taste. Score both systems on the sealed test set with exact-match precision, recall and F1.
  7. Serve. Wrap it in this app so the output can be seen, compared and even scaled.

Tasting notes results on 82 held-out lines

Quantity1.00 vs 0.99

Numbers have a shape. Both systems get them.

Unit0.96 vs 0.99

A short fixed list. The rules win here.

Ingredient0.95 vs 0.81

Open vocabulary and context. This is where learning pays off.

Prep0.81 vs 0.78

Fewest examples, most varied wording. Hardest for both.

Format: model vs regex, F1. Overall 0.95 vs 0.90. One lesson to take away: rules win when the vocabulary is a closed list; learning wins when the words around a token decide its meaning.

Chef's notes limits, said plainly

  • The gold labels were produced by an LLM (Claude) following the written guidelines, with no human review. The labels can be audited in Label Studio.
  • 82 test lines is small. One error moves F1 by about 0.4 points.
  • A known slip: in 2 medium onions the model calls medium a UNIT. It learned the position after a number, not the word.
  • Roman script only, and three recipe sites. Handwritten or social-media recipes may behave differently.
